## The problem

I wanted to build real products instead of treating every project like a
classroom exercise. Qyverix is where that happens — and the thing nobody tells
you is how little of it is writing code.

Shipping client work on a deadline is an operations problem long before it is
an engineering one. Domains, mail, authentication, roles, environments, backups
and logs all have to exist, and then they have to keep existing, and somebody
has to be the reason they do.

## What ownership actually meant

<Decision title="Own the infrastructure" instead="handing off deployment and hoping">
It would have been easier to treat "it works locally" as done and let hosting be
someone else's concern. That is also how you find out at 2 a.m. that nobody
configured mail records, or that a backup has been silently failing for a
fortnight.

Owning the whole path is slower and it is the only version where I learn
anything. "Works on my machine" stopped being a valid engineering strategy the
first time a real client refreshed the page.
</Decision>

The part I am most pleased with is Qyverix Cloud, mostly because it is the kind
of infrastructure layer I wanted to use myself — which turns out to be a
reasonable specification to build against.

## What I learned

Real systems do not care that you are still a student. Nothing about a broken
DNS record is different because the person who owns it has an exam that week.

That sounds like a complaint. It is the opposite — it is the single most useful
thing college could not have taught me, and the reason I stopped treating
deployment as the end of a project rather than the beginning of one.

## What I would change

We said yes to too much variety early on. Every new kind of project meant a new
set of operational edges to maintain, and maintenance compounds in a way that
building does not. Narrower would have been faster.
